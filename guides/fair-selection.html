Was your redundancy fair? Selection, consultation and your options

Last checked: 2 November 2026 · Covers Great Britain. Northern Ireland has separate rules.

The short answer

A redundancy can be genuine and still be handled unfairly. Your employer should choose who goes in a fair and objective way, talk to you about it, and look for other jobs you could do. You can’t be picked for certain reasons, such as your age, disability, pregnancy or trade union membership.

If you think it wasn’t fair, ask for the reasons in writing, appeal in writing, and get free advice early. Time limits apply, and some of the rules are changing between now and January 2027.

What a fair process looks like

  • A fair way of choosing who goes. GOV.UK says your employer should use a fair and objective method of selecting you.
  • Consultation. You are entitled to talk to your employer about why you are being made redundant and about any alternatives.
  • A look at other jobs. If there is suitable alternative work, your employer should offer it to you.
  • A chance to appeal. You can appeal if you feel you were selected unfairly.

How people are chosen

GOV.UK lists the commonly used methods: last in, first out (the shortest-serving employees go first), asking for volunteers, disciplinary records, and staff appraisal scores, skills, qualifications and experience.

  • No selection is needed if your job has disappeared. For example, if a whole operation is closing, or you are the only person doing your job in that part of the organisation.
  • You might be asked to reapply for your own job. If you don’t apply or aren’t successful, you still have a job until your employer makes you redundant.
  • Check “last in, first out” for discrimination, for example if it would mean only younger people are made redundant.

Reasons you cannot be selected for

GOV.UK says that if you are selected for any of these reasons, it counts as unfair dismissal:

  • age, sex, race, disability, religion or belief, sexual orientation, or gender reassignment
  • pregnancy, maternity leave, paternity leave, or parental or dependants leave
  • your working pattern, such as being part-time or on a fixed-term contract
  • trade union membership or non-membership, or lawful industrial action of 12 weeks or less
  • whistleblowing, health and safety activities, jury service, or using your legal rights

GOV.UK’s page has the full list. Ask for advice if any of these might apply, because some of these reasons can be challenged even if you haven’t been in the job long.

Consultation

If your employer is making up to 19 redundancies at the same time, GOV.UK says there are no rules about how they carry out the consultation, but it should still be a real conversation about why it is happening and what alternatives exist.

If they are making 20 or more redundancies at the same time, collective rules apply. Your employer must consult a union representative or an elected employee representative. The consultation must start at least 30 days before any dismissals take effect if 20 to 99 jobs are affected, and at least 45 days before if 100 or more are. The government has said the trigger for collective consultation is expected to change in 2027, so check the current rules.

Other jobs and trial periods

GOV.UK says your redundancy could be unfair dismissal if your employer has suitable alternative work and doesn’t offer it to you. If you are offered a suitable job and unreasonably turn it down, you may lose your right to statutory redundancy pay. You have the right to a 4-week trial period in any alternative job, and if you decide during that time that it isn’t suitable, it doesn’t affect your redundancy pay. You must tell your employer within those 4 weeks.

If you think it was unfair

  1. Ask for the reasons in writing: why you were selected, the criteria used and how you scored.
  2. Appeal in writing. GOV.UK says to write to your employer explaining your reasons.
  3. Get free advice early from Acas, Citizens Advice or your union. They can explain your options for your situation.
  4. Think about a claim. GOV.UK says you may be able to claim to an employment tribunal for unfair dismissal. You usually have to contact Acas for early conciliation first.
  5. Read any settlement agreement carefully. See our checklist on what to check in your redundancy offer.

An unfair process doesn’t guarantee compensation, and outcomes vary, so advice matters.

Time limits and who can claim: rules are changing
