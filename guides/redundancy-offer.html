What to check in your redundancy offer before you sign

Last checked: 25 October 2026 · Covers Great Britain

The short answer

Check the numbers against the official calculator, ask for a written breakdown of what is in the payment, and find out whether you are being asked to sign a settlement agreement. If you are, you must get independent advice first, and Acas says you should normally be given at least 10 calendar days to consider it.

1. Check the redundancy pay figure

Work out your statutory redundancy pay with the GOV.UK calculator and compare it with what you have been offered. Your employer can offer more than the statutory amount, but not less. Our guide to how redundancy pay is calculated and taxed explains the rules and the current caps. Check your contract and any company redundancy policy too, in case you are entitled to enhanced pay.

2. Ask what is in the payment

GOV.UK says a termination payment can include statutory redundancy pay, holiday pay, unpaid wages and company benefits such as bonuses. Ask for a written breakdown so you can see each part. It matters because what you pay tax and National Insurance on depends on what is included.

3. Notice

You must be given a notice period, or be paid instead of working it. The statutory minimum is one week if you have worked for your employer between one month and 2 years, one week for each year if you have worked 2 to 12 years, and 12 weeks if you have worked 12 years or more. Your contract may give you more, and your employer cannot give you less. If you are paid instead of working your notice, GOV.UK says you should get all the basic pay you would have received, and extras such as pension contributions or private health care if they are in your contract.

4. Benefits and your last day

Find out exactly when your employment ends, when your last payment will be made, and what happens to your pension contributions and any benefits such as health insurance or a company car. Make sure you receive your P45 when you leave, because a new employer will need it. Our guide to emergency tax codes after redundancy explains why.

5. Are you being asked to sign a settlement agreement?

Some employers offer a settlement agreement, often alongside voluntary redundancy. It is a legal agreement in which you usually give up the right to bring certain claims in return for a payment. It is voluntary, so you do not have to sign. Before you do:

  • You must get independent advice. It has to be from a qualified lawyer, or a certified and authorised union official or advice centre worker. Employers often contribute towards the cost, but that is not a legal requirement.
  • Take your time. The Acas Code of Practice says a minimum of 10 calendar days should normally be allowed to consider the written terms and get advice, unless both sides agree otherwise.
  • Read what you are giving up, including any confidentiality clause and any restrictions on what you can do after you leave.
  • You can negotiate. An adviser can tell you whether the offer is reasonable.

6. Things you may be entitled to that are not in the offer

  • Time off to look for work. If you have been employed for 2 years by the end of your notice, you are allowed a reasonable amount of time off to look for a job or arrange training. GOV.UK says your employer only has to pay up to 40% of one week’s pay for it.
  • Help finding work. GOV.UK says you can contact the Jobcentre Plus Rapid Response Service if you suspect you will be made redundant, during your notice period, and up to 13 weeks after. There are different services in Scotland and Wales.

7. If something feels wrong

GOV.UK says you must be selected for redundancy in a fair way, and that you can appeal if you feel you were selected unfairly. For advice, contact Acas or Citizens Advice. Tribunal claims have strict time limits, so get advice early rather than late.
