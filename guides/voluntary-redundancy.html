Voluntary or compulsory redundancy: how to weigh up an offer

Last checked: 2 November 2026 · Covers Great Britain

The short answer

Voluntary redundancy means your employer invites people to put themselves forward, often with a bigger payment than the legal minimum. You don’t have to apply. If it is accepted, it is still a redundancy and not a resignation, so you keep your right to statutory redundancy pay and notice.

The decision comes down to three things: how much extra you would get compared with the minimum, how long your money would last, and what you would give up, such as pension contributions and possibly your right to bring claims.

What voluntary redundancy is, and what it isn’t

  • You are under no obligation to volunteer. It is entirely your choice.
  • Your employer can say no. Putting your name forward doesn’t guarantee you can leave, for example if they need your skills.
  • If it is accepted, it counts as a dismissal by reason of redundancy. You are entitled to notice and, if you have 2 years’ service, statutory redundancy pay. Our guide to redundancy pay explains how it is worked out.
  • There are no fixed rules on the extra amount. Employers often offer more than the statutory minimum to encourage volunteers, but unless your contract or a company policy says otherwise, how much is up to them.

Questions to ask before you decide

  1. What is the full offer in writing? Redundancy pay, notice, holiday pay, bonuses, and what happens to benefits and share schemes.
  2. How does it compare with compulsory terms? Work out your statutory redundancy pay with the GOV.UK calculator and see how much extra the offer adds.
  3. What is the deadline, and can you change your mind? You can ask to withdraw before your employment ends, but once an agreement is made, your employer may not have to agree.
  4. Is there a settlement agreement? Enhanced offers are often conditional on signing one, which usually means giving up the right to bring certain claims. You must get independent advice first. See our checklist on what to check in your redundancy offer.
  5. When would it be paid, and what would be taxed? Up to £30,000 of redundancy pay is usually tax-free, but other parts of the payment may not be.

Weighing it up

  • Will the money last until you find work? A payment can look large and still run out sooner than you expect. Use the budget planner and read how much cash to keep accessible.
  • What would you lose? Your pension contributions stop when you leave. If you want to ask about paying part of the payment into a pension, read our pension guide first.
  • How would it affect benefits? A redundancy payment counts towards the savings limits for Universal Credit. See benefits while job hunting.
  • What if you stay? Staying isn’t always safer. Later rounds could be compulsory, and the terms may be less generous. Equally, a good offer isn’t a reason to leave if you don’t want to.
  • How easy would it be to find your next job? It depends on your field and area, so be realistic about how long it could take.

Before you say yes

  • Don’t resign first. Leaving by resignation can mean losing your redundancy rights. Wait until your employer confirms in writing that your redundancy has been accepted.
  • Get everything in writing before you make plans.
  • Take advice if the stakes are high. Acas and Citizens Advice are free, and a union may help if you are a member.
