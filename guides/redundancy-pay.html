How redundancy pay is calculated and taxed

Last checked: 25 October 2026 · Covers Great Britain, for redundancies from 6 April 2026

The short answer

If you are an employee with at least 2 years’ service, you are normally entitled to statutory redundancy pay. It is based on your age, your years of service (up to 20) and your weekly pay (capped at £751), so the most you can get is £22,530. Your employer can pay more, but not less.

Up to £30,000 of redundancy pay is tax-free. Other parts of a termination payment, such as holiday pay, unpaid wages and bonuses, may be taxable.

Who is entitled

You will normally get statutory redundancy pay if you are an employee and have worked for your current employer for 2 years or more. You are not entitled to it if your employer offers to keep you on, or offers suitable alternative work that you refuse without good reason. Being dismissed for misconduct is not redundancy. Northern Ireland has separate rules and figures.

How it is calculated

You get, for each full year of service:

  • half a week’s pay for each year you were under 22
  • one week’s pay for each year you were 22 or older but under 41
  • one and a half weeks’ pay for each year you were 41 or older

Only the last 20 years count. Your weekly pay is the average you earned per week over the 12 weeks before the day you got your redundancy notice. It is based on gross pay (before tax), and guaranteed overtime and contractual bonuses or commission can count towards it.

The weekly pay used in the calculation is capped, however much you earn. The cap is £751 a week and the maximum statutory payment is £22,530 (30 weeks at the cap).

ExampleCalculation
10 full years of service, all at age 41 or over, earning £600 a week10 × 1.5 weeks × £600 = £9,000
Same service, earning £900 a weekCalculated on the £751 cap: 10 × 1.5 × £751 = £11,265

Because the age bands apply to each year of service separately, the GOV.UK calculator is the safest way to check your own figure.

Enhanced (contractual) redundancy pay

Your employer might top up the statutory amount. This is called enhanced or contractual redundancy pay. Check your contract and any company policy, because your employer cannot pay you less than the statutory amount. Our checklist on what to check in your redundancy offer covers what else to look for before you sign.

Tax on redundancy pay

Acas says up to £30,000 of redundancy pay is tax-free, and GOV.UK says statutory redundancy pay under £30,000 is not taxable. If you are made redundant you may get a “termination payment”, which GOV.UK says can include statutory redundancy pay, holiday pay, unpaid wages and company benefits such as bonuses. What you pay tax and National Insurance on depends on what is in your payment, so ask your employer for a written breakdown.

GOV.UK also says you pay tax and National Insurance on any payment you receive instead of working your notice period, which can include part of any severance or enhanced redundancy pay (known as Post-Employment Notice Pay, or PENP), and that your employer pays Class 1A National Insurance on anything over £30,000. If you are wondering whether part of your payment can go into a pension, see our guide to pensions when you leave.

Notice

On top of redundancy pay, your employer should either pay you through your notice period or pay you in lieu of notice. The statutory minimum notice is one week if you have worked for your employer between one month and 2 years, one week for each year if you have worked 2 to 12 years, and 12 weeks if you have worked 12 years or more. Your contract may give you more.

If you are not paid

GOV.UK says you have 6 months from the date your job ends to apply for statutory redundancy pay. If your employer is insolvent, you can apply to the government’s Redundancy Payments Service.
