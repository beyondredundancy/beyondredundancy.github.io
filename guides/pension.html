What happens to your workplace pension when you leave

Last checked: 25 October 2026 · Covers the 2026/27 tax year

The short answer

Your pension stays yours. Contributions stop when you leave, and the money normally stays invested until you decide what to do with it. You can leave it where it is or transfer it.

You may also be able to ask your employer to pay part of your redundancy payment into a pension. GOV.UK says you don’t pay tax or National Insurance on employer contributions to a registered pension scheme made as part of a termination payment, unless they go above your Annual Allowance (£60,000 this tax year). The money is then locked away until you reach the minimum pension age, which is 55 now and rises to 57 from April 2028.

Your existing workplace pension

  • You keep what you have built up. Contributions from you and your employer stop, but your pot stays invested and you can usually still see it online and receive statements.
  • You can leave it, or transfer it to another pension, including a new employer’s scheme. Compare charges and any valuable benefits before moving it.
  • If your pension is a “defined benefit” scheme (it pays a guaranteed income based on your salary and service, sometimes called a final salary scheme), different rules apply. Get advice before transferring out of one.
  • Lost track of an old pension? GOV.UK has a free pension tracing service.

Paying redundancy pay into a pension

What about the first £30,000?

GOV.UK says you do not usually pay tax on the first £30,000 of statutory redundancy pay, enhanced redundancy pay and some non-cash benefits combined, and you pay tax on anything above that. Our guide to redundancy pay explains this.

This matters for pensions, because the tax saving comes from the part that would otherwise be taxed. The first £30,000 is already tax-free, so routing it through a pension doesn’t normally save you tax on that part. Money above £30,000 is where an employer contribution can make a difference. Other sources say that only the taxable part of a redundancy payment counts as earnings when working out how much tax relief you can get on your own contributions, so check this before you rely on a personal contribution.

Things to weigh up

  • The tax saving. It depends on the rate you would otherwise pay on that money. In a tax year with a redundancy payment your income can be unusual, and if your total income passes £100,000 you start to lose your tax-free personal allowance, so check your position.
  • Access. Pension money is locked until the minimum pension age, which is 55 now and 57 from April 2028. It is not a place for money you might need in an emergency.
  • Tax when you take it out. Usually about a quarter of what you take out is tax-free and the rest is taxed as income.
  • The Annual Allowance. It is £60,000 this tax year, and you may be able to use unused allowance from the previous three tax years (called carry forward). Going over it can create a tax charge. If you have already taken taxable money out of a pension, a lower £10,000 limit can apply.
  • Timing. The arrangement generally has to be agreed with your employer before the payment is made, so raise it early. Our checklist on what to check in your redundancy offer is a good time to ask.
  • Your emergency cash. Many people want their cash buffer in place first. Our guide to how much cash to keep accessible covers how to work that out.

Whether paying more into a pension is worth it is a personal decision. For many people the main reason is tax efficiency, and the trade-off is giving up access to the money for years.
