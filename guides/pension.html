What happens to your workplace pension when you leave

Last checked: 25 October 2026 · Covers the 2026/27 tax year

The short answer

Your pension stays yours. Payments into it stop when you leave, but the money stays invested until you decide what to do with it.

You may be able to ask your employer to pay part of your redundancy payment into your pension instead of paying it to you. GOV.UK says you don’t pay tax or National Insurance on this, up to a yearly limit. The money is then locked away until you are 55 (57 from April 2028).

Your existing workplace pension

  • You keep what you have built up. You and your employer stop paying in, but the money stays invested and you can still see it online.
  • You can leave it where it is, or move it to another pension, such as a new employer’s scheme. Compare charges and any valuable benefits before moving it.
  • If it is a “final salary” type pension (it pays a guaranteed income based on your pay and years of service), get advice before moving it, because different rules apply.
  • Lost track of an old pension? GOV.UK has a free service to help you find it.

Paying redundancy pay into a pension

There are two ways to do it:

  1. Your employer pays it in for you. Part of your redundancy payment goes straight into your pension, and you never receive it. GOV.UK says you pay no tax or National Insurance on it, unless it takes you over the yearly limit.
  2. You pay it in yourself. You receive your payment, then put some of it into a pension. You get tax relief on what you pay in, but only up to the higher of what you earned in that tax year or £3,600.

The yearly limit is called the Annual Allowance. It is £60,000 this tax year and covers everything paid into all of your pensions, so most people won’t come near it.

Does the first £30,000 matter?

Yes. GOV.UK says you do not usually pay tax on the first £30,000 of your redundancy payment, and you pay tax on anything above that. See our guide to redundancy pay.

So a pension can only save tax on the part that would otherwise be taxed. For example, if your payment is £40,000, the first £30,000 is already tax-free and the other £10,000 would normally be taxed. Having that £10,000 paid into your pension by your employer could mean it isn’t taxed. Paying the tax-free £30,000 into a pension wouldn’t normally save you any tax.

Is it worth it?

That is a personal decision. For many people the main reason is saving tax. The trade-offs are:

  • It’s locked away. You usually can’t take money out of a pension until you are 55, and 57 from April 2028, so it is not a place for money you might need in an emergency. Our guide to how much cash to keep accessible can help you decide that first.
  • You pay tax when you take it out. Usually about a quarter is tax-free and the rest is taxed as income.
  • Timing. The arrangement generally has to be agreed with your employer before the payment is made, so ask early. Our checklist on what to check in your redundancy offer is a good place to start.
