Where to keep cash savings: easy access, fixed and ISA compared

Last checked: 25 October 2026

The short answer

The right account depends mostly on when you might need the money. Cash you may need soon belongs somewhere you can get at it without a penalty. Money you are sure you won’t need for a while can sometimes earn more if you lock it away. A cash ISA makes the interest tax-free, but it uses part of your yearly ISA allowance.

Whatever you choose, check how much of your money is protected if a bank fails, and how interest outside an ISA is taxed.

The main types of account

TypeHow it worksWatch out for
Easy accessYou can pay in and take money out whenever you like.The interest rate is usually variable, so the provider can change it, and a bonus rate may end after a set period.
Notice accountYou give a set period of notice before you can withdraw.You can’t reach the money straight away, so it is a poor fit for cash you may need at short notice.
Fixed-rateYou lock the money away for a set term and the rate is fixed.Withdrawing early is usually not allowed or comes with a penalty, so check the terms before you commit.
Cash ISAA savings account where the interest is tax-free. It can be easy access or fixed.It uses part of your £20,000 yearly ISA allowance. Not all cash ISAs let you take money out and put it back, so check whether yours is “flexible”.

Our guide to how much cash to keep accessible covers how to decide how much belongs in the first group, and our guide to the ISA allowance explains the ISA limits and the April 2027 changes.

Reading the interest rate

Rates change often, so this guide doesn’t quote any. When you compare accounts, check whether the headline rate includes a bonus that ends after a few months, whether it is fixed or variable, and whether you can pay in more money after you open the account.

Tax on interest

Interest in an ISA is tax-free. Outside an ISA, GOV.UK says your Personal Savings Allowance lets you earn some interest tax-free each tax year: £1,000 if you are a basic rate taxpayer, £500 if you are a higher rate taxpayer and nothing if you are an additional rate taxpayer. Some people with lower incomes can also use a “starting rate for savings” of up to £5,000. The allowances depend on your total income for the tax year, and your income can be different in a year when you leave a job, so check where you stand.

The allowance is per person, not per account, so interest from all your non-ISA accounts counts together.

How your money is protected

The Financial Services Compensation Scheme (FSCS) automatically protects eligible deposits in UK-authorised banks, building societies and credit unions if the firm fails. The limit is £120,000 per eligible person, per firm, and it covers current, savings and fixed-term accounts. Joint accounts are protected up to the same limit for each person.

The limit applies to your total with each firm, not each account, and some brands share a banking licence and count as one firm. If you hold more than the limit with one firm, you could spread money across firms. The FSCS has a free checker on its website.
