How much cash to keep accessible after redundancy

Last checked: 25 October 2026

The short answer

There is no single right amount. The usual starting point is to hold enough cash in an account you can reach quickly to cover your essential outgoings for a set number of months. MoneyHelper, the government-backed guidance service, suggests three to six months.

After redundancy you don’t know how long finding work will take, so many people think about the longer end of that range. How much is right for you depends on your income, your bills and how secure your next step feels.

How much you keep in easy-access accounts is a personal choice. Some people prefer to keep as much as possible within reach during a period of uncertainty. It is also worth thinking about which money you are confident you won’t need for a while, because that part may be able to earn more elsewhere.

A simple way to work it out

  1. Add up your essential monthly outgoings. That means rent or mortgage, utilities, food, transport, insurance, childcare, debt repayments and any other bills you can’t drop.
  2. Subtract income you will still have. That could be a partner’s pay, benefits or part-time earnings. The gap is what your cash needs to cover each month.
  3. Choose how many months to cover. Multiply the monthly gap by that number.
  4. Add a cushion for bills that don’t come monthly, such as car repairs, annual insurance, a boiler service or a new laptop.

Essential outgoings of £1,800 a month, no other income, 3 months of cover£1,800 × 3 = £5,400

What might push your number up or down

  • Other income in the household. A partner’s pay lowers the monthly gap.
  • How long it might take to find work. That depends on your field, your location and the job market, and nobody can predict it with certainty.
  • Your own caution. Three to six months is a rule of thumb, not a limit. Some people choose to hold more, especially after a job loss when it is hard to know how long finding work will take.
  • Debts. MoneyHelper says that if you have credit card debt, an unauthorised overdraft, payday loans, home credit or mortgage arrears, it would be cheaper in the long run to pay those off before building up your emergency fund.
  • Fixed commitments, such as a mortgage, which make your essentials higher.
  • Other money you could reach quickly, such as a tax refund you expect or investments you could sell, though investments can fall in value.

Money you need to reach, and money you can lock away

It can help to split your cash into two groups: what you might need soon, and what you are confident you can leave alone for a set period.

  • Money you might need soon is usually best kept somewhere you can reach it without a penalty. Some people choose to put all of their cash in this group during a period of uncertainty, and that is a reasonable choice.
  • Money you are confident you won’t need for a year or more may be able to earn a better rate of interest in an account where it is locked away, such as a fixed-rate account.

The trade-off is flexibility. Locked-away money usually can’t be withdrawn early, or can only be withdrawn with a penalty, so only lock away what you really won’t need. Our guide to where to keep cash savings compares the options.

Keep it somewhere you can reach it

The point of this money is to be there when you need it, so avoid putting it anywhere that locks it away or charges a penalty to get it out. The usual advice is an instant-access account. Our guide to where to keep cash savings compares the main account types.

How savings can affect benefits

Redundancy pay and savings can matter if you may claim benefits while you look for work.

  • Universal Credit is affected by savings. GOV.UK says that if you have less than £6,000 it doesn’t affect your award. Between £6,000 and £16,000 your payments are reduced by £4.35 for every £250 you have over £6,000. You can usually only claim if you have no more than £16,000 in money, savings and investments.
  • New-style Jobseeker’s Allowance is not means-tested, so savings don’t affect how much you can get. It is based on your National Insurance contributions.

A redundancy payment counts as part of your money if you claim Universal Credit, so it is worth checking the official rules before you make any big decisions.
